About this work

The Ebers Papyrus is the longest and best-preserved medical book to survive from ancient Egypt: a roll of 110 columns written in hieratic around 1550 BC, gathering nearly nine hundred remedies for complaints of the belly, skin, eyes and heart. Its prescriptions draw on a rich materia medica — myrrh, honey, frankincense and the castor plant recur throughout — and its treatise on the vessels sketches the earliest surviving theory of physiology. The library holds the papyrus itself (EB01) alongside Heinrich Joachim’s German translation of 1890 (EB02).

Read more — discovery, contents, significance

The roll was bought at Luxor in 1862 by the American collector Edwin Smith, said to have been found between the legs of a mummy in the Assasif necropolis. Georg Ebers acquired it in 1872 for the Leipzig University Library, published a full facsimile with introduction in 1875, and gave the papyrus its modern name. It has remained at Leipzig ever since, under the shelfmark P. Eb.

The text is a compilation rather than a single treatise: recipe collections against diseases of the belly and worms, an eye-book, a skin-book, gynaecological prescriptions, and the celebrated treatise on the heart and vessels (Eb 854–856), in which channels — the metu — carry air, blood and disease through the body. The numbered remedies name plants, minerals and animal substances with quantities in fractions of the hekat, each new section opened by a rubric in red.

A calendar on the verso naming year 9 of Amenhotep I anchors the roll near 1550 BC, making it a fixed point for Egyptian chronology as well as for the history of medicine. Together with the Edwin Smith surgical papyrus it defines most of what is known of pharaonic medical practice. Joachim’s translation of 1890 — imperfect, but unreplaced in scope until the Grundriss of the 1950s — opened the text to historians of medicine and remains the version through which most readers first meet it.
